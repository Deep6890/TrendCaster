FILE 13: CURRENCY WARS AND EXCHANGE RATES — WHAT THEY MEAN FOR YOU
================================================================
How global currency movements ripple into your household finances
================================================================

WHY EXCHANGE RATES MATTER TO SOMEONE WHO NEVER TRAVELS ABROAD
================================================================
Most people think exchange rates only matter if you are travelling internationally 
or sending money abroad. This is incorrect. Exchange rates affect the prices of 
almost every imported product in India — from the petrol in your vehicle, to the 
edible oil in your kitchen, to the smartphone in your pocket. 

India imports approximately $600-700 billion worth of goods every year. These 
imports are primarily paid for in US dollars. When the rupee weakens, India pays 
more rupees for the same dollar amount — effectively making the entire import bill 
more expensive and transmitting that cost through the economy to consumers.

================================================================
HOW THE RUPEE EXCHANGE RATE IS DETERMINED
================================================================
The rupee-dollar exchange rate is determined primarily by four forces:

Supply and demand for dollars in India: When Indian exporters bring home their 
dollar earnings, they sell dollars (increasing supply, which strengthens the 
rupee). When Indian companies need to pay for imports or repay dollar loans, 
they buy dollars (increasing demand, which weakens the rupee).

Foreign portfolio investment flows: Foreign investors buy Indian stocks and bonds 
using dollars. This creates dollar supply, strengthening the rupee. When they 
sell Indian assets and take money out (as they did aggressively in 2022 when 
the US raised rates), they buy dollars back, weakening the rupee.

RBI intervention: The RBI actively buys and sells dollars from its foreign 
exchange reserves (currently approximately $600-650 billion, one of the world's 
largest) to prevent excessive rupee volatility. If the rupee is falling too fast, 
the RBI sells dollars from reserves to add supply and slow the decline. This 
"managed float" is India's approach — not fixed, not fully free-floating.

Global dollar strength cycle: The US Federal Reserve's interest rate decisions 
affect the value of the dollar against all currencies simultaneously. When the 
Fed raised rates aggressively in 2022-2023, the dollar strengthened against 
nearly every major currency, including the rupee — regardless of India-specific 
factors.

================================================================
WHAT IS A CURRENCY WAR?
================================================================
A currency war is when multiple countries deliberately try to weaken their own 
currencies to gain competitive advantage for exports. This sounds paradoxical — 
why would a country want a weaker currency? The answer lies in trade economics.

When Country A's currency weakens:
  - Country A's exports become cheaper for foreign buyers (priced in weaker 
    currency = less expensive in foreign currency terms)
  - Country A's imports become more expensive (weakens domestic purchasing power)
  - Country A's manufacturers get an export advantage

This was a persistent theme in global economics from 2010-2020, as the US, Europe, 
and Japan all pursued ultra-loose monetary policies (low rates, money printing) that 
weakened their currencies and boosted export competitiveness. China has also been 
accused of deliberately managing the yuan below fair value to support its export economy.

The danger of currency wars: If everyone devalues simultaneously, nobody gains a 
competitive advantage — but everyone suffers higher inflation as import costs rise 
for all countries. It is a race to the bottom that generates inflation globally 
without producing genuine economic growth.

================================================================
THE DE-DOLLARIZATION TREND — WHAT IT MEANS
================================================================
De-dollarization refers to the gradual, ongoing shift by some countries away from 
using the US dollar as the primary currency for international trade and reserves. 
This is one of the most significant long-term economic trends of the 2020s.

The context: After the US froze Russia's dollar-denominated foreign exchange reserves 
in 2022 (in response to the Ukraine invasion), many non-Western countries took 
note. If the US could simply freeze access to another country's reserves held in 
dollars, then holding large dollar reserves is a geopolitical risk, not just an 
economic choice.

What is happening practically:
  - Russia and China are conducting bilateral trade in yuan and rubles, bypassing 
    dollar payment systems
  - India and Russia have traded in rupees for oil purchases
  - The BRICS group (Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa, now expanded) is 
    actively discussing alternative payment systems and reserve currencies
  - Central banks globally have been reducing the dollar share of their reserves 
    and increasing gold holdings since 2022

Implications for India: India has a complex position. On one hand, reducing dollar 
dependence could give India more monetary autonomy and shield it from dollar 
fluctuations. On the other hand, the rupee is not yet a reserve currency, and 
switching trade to rupees requires other countries to want to hold rupees — which 
requires India's economy and currency to be seen as stable and trustworthy.

The practical impact for ordinary Indians: De-dollarization is a slow-moving trend 
that will play out over decades. Its near-term impact on your household is limited. 
But over 20-30 years, if the dollar weakens structurally as the world's reserve 
currency, the relative value of rupee-denominated assets (your PPF, your property, 
your Indian equities) improves against dollar benchmarks.

================================================================
PRACTICAL EXCHANGE RATE STRATEGIES FOR NORMAL INDIANS
================================================================
If you send remittances abroad (for family, education, or any purpose), the 
exchange rate affects how much value you transfer. Here are practical steps:

Compare rates across platforms: The exchange rate offered by your bank is usually 
0.5% to 2% worse than the mid-market rate (the rate you see on Google). Apps 
like Wise (TransferWise), Remitly, and Western Money are often 40-70% cheaper 
than traditional bank wire transfers for international remittances. For a ₹5 lakh 
international transfer, this cost difference can be ₹2,000 to ₹8,000.

For education payments abroad: If you are funding a child's foreign university 
education, forward contracts (locking in today's exchange rate for a future 
payment) can protect you from rupee depreciation between now and the payment 
date. Ask your bank about forex forward contracts for education expenses.

For NRIs remitting to India: When the rupee weakens significantly (as it did in 
2022-2023), it is actually a favorable time for NRIs to send money to India — 
you get more rupees per dollar, strengthening family support or investment returns 
in rupee-denominated assets.

================================================================
THE RUPEE'S LONG-TERM TRAJECTORY — HONEST ANALYSIS
================================================================
The rupee has depreciated against the dollar over every decade in modern history:
  1990: ₹17.5 per dollar
  2000: ₹45 per dollar
  2010: ₹45 per dollar (decade of stability)
  2020: ₹75 per dollar
  2024: ₹83-87 per dollar

This structural depreciation reflects India's higher inflation rate compared to 
the US. India's inflation has averaged 5-7% annually versus the US's 2-3%. The 
theory of Purchasing Power Parity (PPP) predicts that a currency's exchange rate 
should depreciate at roughly the inflation differential over the long term. For 
India vs US, that is approximately 3-4% annual rupee depreciation — which matches 
the observed long-term trend.

What this means for your investments: Any India-only investment portfolio is 
subject to this long-term currency depreciation when measured in dollar terms. 
This is one argument for holding some international (USD-denominated) assets — 
not as speculation, but as a structural currency hedge that compensates for the 
rupee's predictable long-term weakness.
