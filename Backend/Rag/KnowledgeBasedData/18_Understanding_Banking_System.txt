FILE 18: HOW THE BANKING SYSTEM WORKS — EXPLAINED FOR EVERYONE

Why banks fail, how your money is protected, and what you must know
================================================================

HOW BANKS ACTUALLY WORK (THE SURPRISING REALITY)
================================================================
Most people believe banks store their deposited money in a vault and lend out other 
people's money. This mental model is wrong in an important way. Banks operate on 
a fractional reserve system — when you deposit ₹1,000 in a bank, the bank keeps 
only a small fraction (the Cash Reserve Ratio, currently 4% in India) and loans out 
the rest to other borrowers. Those borrowers spend the money, it gets deposited 
elsewhere, and that bank in turn keeps a fraction and lends out the rest. The same 
original deposit travels through the system multiple times, with the banking system 
as a whole creating far more credit than the original deposits would suggest.

This is called the money multiplier effect. ₹100 deposited in the banking system 
can result in ₹1,000 to ₹2,500 of total lending through the chain — depending on 
the reserve ratio and how efficiently banks lend. The RBI manages this process 
through the Cash Reserve Ratio (CRR) and Statutory Liquidity Ratio (SLR), which 
together determine how much of every deposit banks must hold back rather than lend.

Understanding this explains why bank runs are so dangerous. If enough depositors 
simultaneously demand their money back, the bank cannot physically produce it 
because it has been lent out. This is precisely what happened to Silicon Valley Bank 
in the US in 2023 — a classic bank run in the digital age, where word spread on 
social media and billions were withdrawn within hours.

HOW YOUR MONEY IS PROTECTED IN INDIA
================================================================
DICGC (Deposit Insurance and Credit Guarantee Corporation) insures bank deposits 
up to ₹5 lakh per depositor per bank. This means if a bank fails, you are guaranteed 
to receive back up to ₹5 lakh of your total deposits at that bank — irrespective of 
which type of account (savings, FD, current, RD).

Critical points about this insurance: The ₹5 lakh limit is per depositor per bank — 
not per account. If you have ₹3 lakh in a savings account and ₹4 lakh in FDs at the 
same bank, your total insured amount is ₹5 lakh, not ₹7 lakh. To protect amounts 
above ₹5 lakh, you must spread deposits across multiple banks.

Practical implication for risk management: If you have significant savings (above 
₹10-15 lakh), distribute them across at least two to three banks. Keep ₹5 lakh or 
less per bank beyond what you need for day-to-day banking. This simple step fully 
protects your deposits against any single bank failure, regardless of the bank's 
financial health.

The relative safety hierarchy of banks in India (from safest to higher-risk):
  Scheduled commercial banks (SBI, HDFC, ICICI, Kotak): Highest safety. 
  Most have very low probability of failure. SBI is effectively government-backed.
  
  Small Finance Banks (AU, ESAF, Jana, etc.): RBI-regulated but smaller. 
  Offer higher interest rates because they carry marginally more risk. The ₹5 lakh 
  DICGC insurance applies here too. Limiting deposits to ₹5 lakh per small finance 
  bank is prudent.
  
  Cooperative banks: Have historically had higher failure rates in India. 
  The 2019 PMC Bank crisis (where depositors were locked out of their own money) 
  is a cautionary example. Keep exposures to cooperative banks minimal.
  
  Non-Banking Financial Companies (NBFCs): These are NOT banks. Deposits in 
  NBFCs (some accept public deposits) are NOT covered by DICGC insurance. They can 
  offer higher rates but carry higher risk. Limit NBFC deposits to small amounts 
  you can afford to lose in a worst case.

THE RBI'S ROLE — INDIA'S ECONOMIC GUARDIAN
================================================================
The Reserve Bank of India functions as: the nation's central bank, the regulator 
of commercial banks, the issuer of currency, the manager of India's foreign 
exchange reserves, and the conductor of monetary policy. Understanding what the 
RBI does and how it makes decisions helps you anticipate economic changes that 
directly affect your household.

The RBI Monetary Policy Committee (MPC) meets 6 times a year and makes two 
key decisions each time. First, whether to raise, lower, or hold the repo rate 
(the rate at which RBI lends to banks). Second, the policy stance — whether the 
MPC is focused more on fighting inflation ("withdrawal of accommodation") or 
supporting growth ("accommodative"). The stance signals the direction of future 
rate moves even when the rate itself does not change that meeting.

The RBI also regulates bank health through the following tools. The CRR (Cash 
Reserve Ratio), currently 4%, requires banks to hold that percentage of deposits 
as cash with the RBI — earning zero interest. This limits the money supply. The 
SLR (Statutory Liquidity Ratio), currently 18%, requires banks to hold that 
percentage in liquid government securities. Together, CRR + SLR = 22% of deposits 
held back from lending. When the RBI raises CRR, less money is available for 
lending — a tightening move.

WHAT TRIGGERS A BANKING CRISIS AND THE WARNING SIGNS
================================================================
Banking crises typically have four precursors. The first is rapid expansion of 
credit without adequate risk assessment — banks lending aggressively during boom 
periods to borrowers who would not qualify in normal times. The second is 
concentration risk — a bank having too much exposure to a single sector (real 
estate, for instance) or a single large borrower group. The third is asset-liability 
mismatch — borrowing short (accepting 1-3 year deposits) while lending long 
(giving 15-20 year home loans). The fourth is poor governance — management taking 
excessive risks, connected lending to promoters or insiders, fraudulent accounting.

India's banking sector went through its own NPA (Non-Performing Assets) crisis 
between 2015 and 2021, when bad loan ratios at government-owned banks peaked 
at over 11% — meaning 11 rupees of every 100 rupees lent was not being repaid. 
This required significant recapitalization by the government (using taxpayer money) 
and forced consolidation of public sector banks.

Warning signs that a bank may be in distress (applicable to consumers monitoring 
their bank's health): Consistently high NPA ratios (check the bank's annual report 
or banking news), frequent management changes or regulatory interventions, 
difficulty accessing deposits or slow processing (an early warning of liquidity 
stress), RBI imposing restrictions on withdrawals (the ultimate warning sign — 
though by this point it is too late to act).

The practical consumer response: Maintain diversification across banks, stay within 
DICGC limits at any single bank, and monitor RBI news for any regulatory action 
on banks where you hold significant deposits.

HOW DIGITAL BANKING AND UPI HAVE CHANGED RISK
================================================================
The digitization of Indian banking through UPI, internet banking, and mobile apps 
has created enormous convenience but also introduced new risks that did not exist 
in the era of branch-only banking.

The specific risks every digital banking user faces: Phishing attacks (fraudulent 
messages or calls impersonating bank officials or government agencies), SIM-swap 
fraud (fraudsters convincing mobile carriers to issue them a new SIM for your 
number, then taking over your 2FA-protected accounts), vishing (voice phishing — 
calls that seem legitimate asking for OTPs or card details), and UPI payment fraud 
(requests to "collect" money that actually debit your account).

The most important protective rule is absolute: No bank, government agency, or 
legitimate company will ever ask for your OTP, PIN, CVV number, or full card 
details over the phone or via a link. Any such request is 100% a fraud attempt, 
with zero exceptions. This rule applies even if the caller has partial information 
about you (which fraudsters can obtain from data leaks). Never share OTPs or PIN 
under any circumstances.
