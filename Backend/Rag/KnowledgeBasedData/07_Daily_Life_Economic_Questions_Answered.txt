Q: SHOULD I BUY A HOME OR KEEP RENTING? (THE MOST ASKED QUESTION)
-------------------------------------------------------------------
This depends on three variables that are specific to your situation: your city, 
your income stability, and your time horizon. There is no universal right answer.

The "Buy" case is strong when:
- The ratio of annual rent to property purchase price is above 4% (so ₹12,000/month 
  rent on a property worth ₹30 lakh = 4.8% yield — buying makes mathematical sense)
- You plan to stay in the city for at least 7-10 years
- You can afford a 20% down payment without depleting your emergency fund
- Your total EMI is not more than 30-35% of your take-home pay

The "Rent" case is better when:
- You are in a high-rent city where property prices are 50-80x annual rent (paying 
  ₹30,000/month rent for a flat worth ₹3 crore = only 1.2% yield — owning is 
  significantly more expensive than renting)
- Your job requires mobility
- You are early in your career and your income may change significantly in 3-5 years
- Local property market has already seen large recent appreciation

Simple check: If the property you want to buy costs ₹60 lakh and market rent for 
a similar property is ₹18,000/month, rent is ₹2.16 lakh/year. Your EMI on a 
₹48 lakh loan (80% financing, 20% down) at 9% for 20 years would be approximately 
₹43,200/month or ₹5.18 lakh/year. You are paying ₹3 lakh more per year to own 
rather than rent — that extra ₹3 lakh invested in PPF or equity funds for 10 years 
at 10% return becomes approximately ₹52 lakh. However, you miss any property price 
appreciation. This calculation needs to be done fresh for your specific situation.

Q: IS GOLD JEWELLERY A GOOD INVESTMENT?
-------------------------------------------------------------------
Gold jewellery is a sentimental asset and a cultural necessity — but as a pure 
financial investment, it is inefficient. The reasons are specific and real.

Making charges at jewellers range from 8% to 25% of gold value. These are paid 
upfront and are not recovered at resale (most jewellers buy back at melt value — 
pure metal price, no making charge credit). A hallmarked ring bought for ₹50,000 
with 20% making charges contains only ₹41,667 worth of gold. To break even, gold 
must rise 20% before you have even started earning a return.

Additionally, purity is often uncertain in older jewellery. 22 karat vs 18 karat 
makes a significant difference in gold content.

If you are buying jewellery for personal use, wearing occasions, or as a gift — that 
is entirely valid. But account for it as a consumption cost, not an investment. For 
investment purposes, sovereign gold bonds (zero making charges, tax-free at maturity, 
earns 2.5% interest) are strictly superior.

Q: WHAT IS REPO RATE AND HOW DOES IT AFFECT MY HOME LOAN EMI?
-------------------------------------------------------------------
The repo rate is the interest rate at which the RBI lends money to commercial banks. 
When the RBI raises this rate, banks' borrowing cost increases, so they raise rates 
on home loans, personal loans, and car loans. When RBI cuts repo rate, your floating 
rate loan EMI goes down.

Example: You have a ₹40 lakh floating rate home loan at 9%. RBI cuts repo rate by 
0.50% (50 basis points). Your bank passes this on (usually with a lag of 1-3 months). 
Your rate drops to 8.5%. On a 20-year outstanding loan, this reduces your EMI from 
approximately ₹35,988 to ₹34,812 — saving ₹1,176/month or ₹14,112/year.

Over the remaining loan period, a 0.5% cut on ₹40 lakh for 20 years saves you 
approximately ₹2.8 lakh in total interest. Rate cycles matter significantly.

Q: WHAT IS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN SAVINGS AND INVESTMENT?
-------------------------------------------------------------------
Savings: Money set aside for short-term security and accessibility. Goes into savings 
accounts, liquid funds, FDs with short maturity. Purpose is to PROTECT the money and 
ensure it is available when needed. Returns are low (3-7%) but the money is safe.

Investment: Money committed for a longer term with an expectation of growth that beats 
inflation. Goes into equities, bonds, real estate, gold. Purpose is to GROW the money 
beyond inflation. Returns can be higher (8-15%+) but carry some risk of loss.

The key distinction: You should never "invest" your emergency fund, and you should 
never "save" your long-term wealth in low-return instruments. Most people get this 
exactly backwards — they keep too much in savings accounts (thinking it's safe) while 
their long-term wealth grows below inflation.

Q: WHAT IS A CREDIT SCORE AND WHY DOES IT MATTER?
-------------------------------------------------------------------
A credit score (CIBIL score in India) is a number between 300 and 900 that represents 
your creditworthiness — how reliably you have repaid loans and credit card bills in 
the past. A score above 750 is generally considered good. Above 800 is excellent.

Why it matters practically:
- A good score (750+) means you get home loans at the best available interest rates.
- A poor score (below 650) can result in loan rejection or rates 2-3% higher than 
  the best rate — which on a ₹40 lakh home loan over 20 years means paying lakhs 
  more in interest.
- A score below 600 can even affect job applications at financial companies or for 
  roles requiring financial trustworthiness.

What damages your credit score: Missing any EMI or credit card payment (even once), 
using more than 30-40% of your total credit card limit consistently, having too many 
loan applications in a short period.

What builds your credit score: Paying every EMI on time without exception, keeping 
credit card utilisation below 30%, maintaining old credit accounts even if unused.

Q: WHAT IS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN INCOME TAX SLABS — OLD VS NEW REGIME?
-------------------------------------------------------------------
India currently has two parallel income tax regimes for salaried individuals.

Old Regime: Higher tax rates but many deductions available. You can reduce your 
taxable income by claiming: Section 80C (PPF, ELSS, life insurance — up to ₹1.5 lakh), 
HRA (house rent allowance), home loan interest (Section 24 — up to ₹2 lakh), NPS 
(Section 80CCD — additional ₹50,000), health insurance (Section 80D — up to ₹25,000), 
and others. Total deductions can reduce taxable income by ₹4-5 lakh for a disciplined 
investor.

New Regime: Lower slab rates but minimal deductions allowed. Starting FY 2024-25, 
income up to ₹7 lakh has zero tax under the new regime (after standard deduction and 
rebate). The slabs are more gradual and simpler.

Which to choose: If you actively invest in PPF, have a home loan, pay rent and claim 
HRA, and contribute to NPS — the old regime likely saves more tax. If you do not have 
these deductions (young earner, renting without HRA, no active tax-saving investments), 
the new regime is simpler and often saves more tax. Calculate both scenarios before 
filing each year.

Q: WHAT IS INFLATION'S REAL IMPACT ON MY ₹10,000 SAVINGS?
-------------------------------------------------------------------
At 6% annual inflation, ₹10,000 today buys the same goods as ₹10,000. In 10 years, 
you will need ₹17,908 to buy the same goods. That means if your ₹10,000 earns only 
3-4% in a savings account, after 10 years your ₹13,000-14,000 balance buys LESS 
than your original ₹10,000 could today. You are poorer in real terms despite having 
nominally more money.

This is why simply saving is not enough. You must invest at rates that beat inflation 
after tax. The minimum bar is: post-tax return must exceed inflation rate. For someone 
in the 20% tax bracket earning 7% on an FD, after-tax return is 5.6% — barely above 
6% inflation. For a 30% bracket taxpayer, after-tax return is 4.9% — clearly below 
inflation. This is the mathematical case for equity and PPF investing.
