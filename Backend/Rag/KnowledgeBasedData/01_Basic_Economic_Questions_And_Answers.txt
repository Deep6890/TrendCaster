FILE 01: BASIC ECONOMIC QUESTIONS EVERY NORMAL PERSON ASKS
================================================================
Last Updated Reference: 2024-2025 Global Economic Context
Target Audience: General Public — No Finance Background Required

QUESTION 1: WHY ARE PRICES GOING UP EVEN THOUGH I EARN THE SAME?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
This is called inflation. Inflation means the purchasing power of your money decreases 
over time. If bread cost ₹30 last year and now costs ₹38, that 26% rise is inflation 
affecting your daily life. The core reason is that when more money circulates in an 
economy (due to government spending, loans, or printing money) but the supply of goods 
stays the same, each unit of money becomes worth less. Think of it like this: if there 
are 10 apples and 100 rupees chasing them, each apple costs ₹10. If suddenly 200 
rupees are in circulation but still only 10 apples exist, each apple now costs ₹20. 
Your salary did not go up, but the goods did — that gap is what hurts you.

In India's context (2024-2025), food inflation has been driven by erratic monsoons 
affecting vegetable supply, higher fuel costs pushing transport prices up, and global 
commodity price rises feeding into local retail prices.

WHAT YOU CAN DO: Track your monthly spending on 5 core categories — food, transport, 
utilities, rent, and healthcare. If any one of them rises more than 8% year-over-year, 
you need to either cut spending in another category or look for an income supplement.

----------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION 2: WHY DOES THE GOVERNMENT RAISE INTEREST RATES WHEN PRICES GO UP?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
The Reserve Bank of India (RBI) or any central bank raises interest rates to make 
borrowing expensive. When borrowing is expensive, people and businesses take fewer 
loans. Fewer loans mean less money flowing in the economy. Less money chasing the same 
goods means prices stabilize. It is a deliberate "cooling" of the economy.

The side effect is that your home loan EMI goes up. A 1% rate hike on a ₹40 lakh home 
loan over 20 years increases your EMI by roughly ₹2,200 to ₹2,800 per month. This is 
real money out of a household budget.

WHAT YOU CAN DO: If you have a floating-rate loan, during a rate-hike cycle consider 
switching to a fixed rate to protect yourself. If rates are at a peak (which they were 
in 2023-2024), wait — they will eventually come down, and your floating EMI will 
reduce on its own.

----------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION 3: WHAT IS GDP AND WHY DO PEOPLE KEEP TALKING ABOUT IT?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
GDP (Gross Domestic Product) is the total value of all goods and services produced in 
a country in one year. Think of it as the country's annual report card. If India's GDP 
grows at 7%, it means the economy produced 7% more value this year than last year. 
This generally means more jobs, more business activity, and in theory, more prosperity 
for people.

But GDP growth does not automatically mean you personally are better off. If the 
growth is concentrated in a few sectors (like software or manufacturing) or in a few 
cities, you may not feel it in your pocket. GDP is an average — and averages hide 
extremes.

India's GDP (2024-2025): India is growing at approximately 6.5% to 7%, making it one 
of the fastest-growing major economies. However, rural income growth, small business 
recovery, and wage growth for lower-income groups are growing slower than the headline 
number suggests.

----------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION 4: WHAT IS THE DIFFERENCE BETWEEN RECESSION AND DEPRESSION?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
A recession is when the economy shrinks (negative GDP growth) for two or more 
consecutive quarters — roughly 6 months. People lose jobs, businesses cut spending, 
and consumer confidence falls. The 2008 global financial crisis triggered recessions 
in most developed economies.

A depression is a severe, prolonged recession lasting years with unemployment exceeding 
20% and GDP dropping more than 10%. The last true global depression was in the 1930s.

A slowdown (what India sometimes experiences) is growth that is lower than expected 
but still positive. For example, growth dropping from 8% to 5% is a slowdown, not a 
recession. Still painful, still leads to job cuts and reduced hiring — but the economy 
is not shrinking.

WHAT THIS MEANS FOR YOU: During a slowdown or recession, the safest personal strategy 
is to reduce debt, build an emergency fund, and avoid major financial commitments like 
buying property at peak prices.

QUESTION 5: WHY DOES THE DOLLAR RATE AFFECT MY DAILY LIFE IN INDIA?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
India imports massive quantities of crude oil, electronics, edible oils, and 
fertilizers — all priced in US dollars. When the rupee weakens against the dollar 
(say, from ₹82 to ₹86 per dollar), India has to pay more rupees for the same barrel 
of oil. This extra cost gets passed on to you as higher petrol prices, higher diesel 
prices, and then higher transport costs — which makes vegetables, grains, and consumer 
goods cost more. A weak rupee is like a hidden tax on every imported product you use.

Conversely, a stronger rupee makes imports cheaper and can ease inflation.

Current Context (2024-2025): The rupee has been hovering between ₹83–₹87 to the 
dollar. This is a historically weak zone and is one of the reasons fuel and imported 
goods remain expensive even when global crude oil prices moderate.

----------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION 6: WHAT IS FISCAL DEFICIT AND DOES IT AFFECT ME?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Fiscal deficit is the gap between what the government earns (taxes, fees) and what it 
spends (salaries, subsidies, infrastructure). If the government collects ₹20 lakh 
crore in revenue but spends ₹25 lakh crore, the ₹5 lakh crore gap is the fiscal 
deficit. The government borrows to cover this gap — usually by issuing bonds.

The danger is that heavy government borrowing "crowds out" private borrowing. Banks 
prefer lending to the government (safe, guaranteed return) over lending to small 
businesses (riskier). This can slow down small business lending and economic 
opportunity for ordinary citizens.

India's fiscal deficit target is around 4.5% to 5% of GDP (2024-2025 budget). The 
government has been trying to narrow it to reduce long-term economic instability.

----------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION 7: WHY DO SOME COUNTRIES PRINT MONEY AND OTHERS CANNOT?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
The United States can print dollars because the dollar is the world's reserve currency 
— most global trade happens in dollars. So even when the US prints more dollars, 
global demand absorbs the excess. Countries like India, Argentina, or Sri Lanka cannot 
do this freely because their currency is not globally demanded. If India prints excess 
rupees, the rupee simply weakens against the dollar, imports become more expensive, 
and inflation spikes rapidly. This is why currency management by the RBI is critical 
for India's economic stability.

----------------------------------------------------------------

QUESTION 8: WHAT IS STAGFLATION AND IS IT HAPPENING NOW?
-----------------------------------------------------------------
Stagflation is the worst economic situation: high inflation AND slow growth at the 
same time. Normally, inflation happens when economies are growing fast (too much 
demand). But stagflation breaks that pattern. It happened in the 1970s globally due to 
oil shocks.

The current global environment (2023-2025) has had mild stagflation risk in many 
developed economies — inflation stayed high while growth slowed. India avoided the 
worst of this due to its domestic demand base and relatively insulated economy, but 
was not fully immune given rising import costs.
