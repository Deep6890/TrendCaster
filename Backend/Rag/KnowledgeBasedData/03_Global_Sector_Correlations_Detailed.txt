UNDERSTANDING CORRELATIONS FIRST
================================================================
A correlation tells you how two assets move relative to each other.
  Positive correlation = they move in the same direction
  Negative correlation = they move in opposite directions
  Zero correlation = they move independently

In economics, nothing is perfectly correlated forever. Correlations shift based on 
global conditions, geopolitical events, and monetary policy cycles. What this file 
provides is the typical, historically observed behaviour and why it exists.

================================================================
SECTOR 1: GOLD (XAU/USD)
================================================================

WHAT MOVES GOLD UP:
  - When the US dollar weakens: Gold is priced in USD globally. When the dollar falls, 
    gold becomes cheaper for foreign buyers, demand increases, price rises. This is the 
    most consistent driver of gold prices over time.

  - When inflation rises: Gold has been used as a store of value for thousands of years. 
    When people fear paper currency is losing purchasing power, they shift to gold as a 
    hedge. In 2022-2023, when global inflation hit 40-year highs, gold stayed elevated 
    even as interest rates rose.

  - When geopolitical uncertainty rises: Wars, banking crises, political instability — 
    all push investors toward gold as a "safe haven" asset. The Russia-Ukraine war in 
    2022 and the Middle East conflict in 2023 both triggered gold price spikes.

  - When real interest rates are negative: Real interest rate = Nominal rate - Inflation. 
    When inflation exceeds the interest you earn on bonds, gold (which earns nothing but 
    holds value) becomes relatively attractive. This is the key mechanism.

  - When central banks buy gold: Central banks of Russia, China, India, Turkey, and 
    several other countries have been aggressively buying gold since 2022, partly to 
    diversify away from USD. This institutional demand provides a price floor.

WHAT MOVES GOLD DOWN:
  - Rising real interest rates: When bond yields rise faster than inflation, investors 
    prefer earning returns on bonds over holding "inert" gold. The aggressive US Federal 
    Reserve rate hikes in 2022-2023 temporarily capped gold's rise despite high inflation.

  - Strong US dollar: A strong dollar makes gold expensive for non-dollar buyers, 
    reducing global demand.

  - Reduced fear/uncertainty: When geopolitical risks ease, safe-haven demand fades.

CURRENT STATUS (2024-2025): Gold has been in a structural bull market, reaching 
all-time highs above $2,400 to $2,700 per troy ounce. Drivers include central bank 
buying, geopolitical instability, US debt concerns, and expectations of eventual 
US Federal Reserve rate cuts. In Indian rupee terms, gold prices have been above 
₹70,000 to ₹85,000 per 10 grams — historically expensive.

GOLD'S CORRELATION WITH OTHER ASSETS:
  Gold vs USD: Typically NEGATIVE. Dollar up = Gold down (and vice versa)
  Gold vs Equities: WEAKLY NEGATIVE to NEUTRAL. In severe crashes, gold rises as stocks fall.
  Gold vs Oil: WEAKLY POSITIVE. Both respond to inflation, but oil is more economic-cycle driven.
  Gold vs Bonds: NEGATIVE when rates rise, POSITIVE when fear dominates markets.

SECTOR 2: CRUDE OIL (Brent / WTI)
================================================================

WHAT MOVES OIL UP:
  - Supply cuts by OPEC+: The Organization of Petroleum Exporting Countries, now expanded 
    to include Russia (OPEC+), controls approximately 40% of global supply. When OPEC+ 
    announces production cuts, supply tightens and prices rise. Saudi Arabia and Russia 
    have repeatedly coordinated cuts in 2023-2024, supporting prices above $80/barrel.

  - Geopolitical disruptions: Any conflict in major oil-producing regions (Middle East, 
    Russia, North Africa) creates supply uncertainty, triggering price spikes. The 
    Russia-Ukraine war in 2022 sent Brent crude to over $120/barrel briefly.

  - Rising global demand: When the global economy grows strongly (especially China and 
    India, the two largest import markets), oil demand rises. Post-COVID reopening in 
    2021-2022 caused a significant demand surge.

  - Weak dollar: Since oil is priced in USD globally, a weaker dollar makes oil cheaper 
    for non-dollar buyers, increasing demand and therefore price.

  - Low inventory levels: When global crude oil inventories fall below historical averages, 
    any supply disruption creates outsized price moves.

WHAT MOVES OIL DOWN:
  - Recession or economic slowdown: Less economic activity = less energy demand. In 2020, 
    oil briefly went negative (an unprecedented event) due to COVID lockdowns eliminating 
    demand while supply continued.

  - OPEC overproduction or quota violations: When OPEC members cheat on quotas or a 
    price war breaks out (as happened between Saudi Arabia and Russia in 2020), massive 
    oversupply crashes prices.

  - Strong USD: Makes oil more expensive for international buyers, reducing demand.

  - Rise of renewables: Longer-term structural factor. As electric vehicles, solar, and 
    wind energy grow, the long-term demand outlook for oil weakens. This is not a 2025 
    story but is relevant over 10-20 years.

  - Strategic petroleum reserve releases: The US and allied countries releasing emergency 
    reserves can temporarily suppress prices (as the US did in 2022).

CURRENT STATUS (2024-2025): Brent crude has been trading in a range of $75-$90/barrel. 
OPEC+ cuts have supported the floor but global demand growth has been moderate due to 
China's slower-than-expected post-COVID recovery and high interest rates dampening 
global economic activity.

OIL'S EFFECT ON INDIA SPECIFICALLY:
India imports 85% of its crude oil requirements. Every $10/barrel increase in oil 
prices costs India approximately $14-15 billion in additional import costs annually. 
This widens the current account deficit, weakens the rupee, raises petrol and diesel 
prices, increases transport costs, and feeds into general inflation. The entire Indian 
economy is highly sensitive to global crude prices.

OIL'S CORRELATIONS WITH OTHER ASSETS:
  Oil vs USD: NEGATIVE. Dollar strengthens → Oil more expensive for others → Demand falls → Oil price falls.
  Oil vs Inflation: STRONGLY POSITIVE. Oil price rises directly feed into CPI data.
  Oil vs Equities: MIXED. In normal times, positive (growth drives both). In supply-shock scenarios, 
    negative (oil rises due to supply crisis, stocks fall due to growth fears).
  Oil vs Gold: WEAKLY POSITIVE. Both benefit from inflation and dollar weakness.

SECTOR 3: US DOLLAR INDEX (DXY)
================================================================
The DXY measures the USD against a basket of 6 major currencies 
(Euro 57.6%, JPY 13.6%, GBP 11.9%, CAD 9.1%, SEK 4.2%, CHF 3.6%).

WHAT MAKES THE DOLLAR STRONGER:
  - US interest rates rising faster than other countries: When the Federal Reserve raises 
    rates while Europe or Japan hold steady, global capital flows into US assets to earn 
    higher returns. This increased demand for dollars pushes the DXY up.

  - Global risk-off events: In moments of global panic (financial crises, wars, pandemics), 
    investors flee to the most liquid, most trusted currency. The dollar is that currency. 
    This "flight to safety" strengthens the USD even when the crisis originates in the US.

  - Strong US economic data: Higher US GDP growth, low unemployment, strong consumer 
    spending — all make the US more attractive for foreign investment, raising dollar demand.

WHAT WEAKENS THE DOLLAR:
  - Federal Reserve cutting rates or signalling dovish policy
  - US fiscal problems (rising debt, deficit spending, credit rating downgrades)
  - De-dollarization moves by major economies (China, Russia, BRICS countries moving 
    to bilateral trade in non-USD currencies — a slow but real trend)
  - Other central banks raising rates faster than the Fed

DOLLAR'S EFFECT ON EMERGING MARKETS (INCLUDING INDIA):
When the dollar is strong, emerging market currencies like the rupee, Turkish lira, 
and Brazilian real typically weaken. This is because:
  1. Investors pull money out of emerging markets to invest in higher-yielding US assets.
  2. Dollar-denominated debt held by emerging market governments and companies becomes 
     more expensive to repay in local currency terms.
  3. Commodity import costs rise (oil, metals — all priced in USD).

A strong dollar period is generally painful for India, raising import costs and putting 
downward pressure on the rupee.

SECTOR 4: EQUITY MARKETS (STOCK MARKETS)
================================================================

WHAT DRIVES MARKETS UP BROADLY:
  - Strong corporate earnings growth
  - Low interest rates (makes future earnings more valuable in discounted cash flow models)
  - Economic expansion and rising employment
  - Technological innovation creating new sectors
  - Central bank quantitative easing (money printing that flows into asset markets)
  - Investor optimism and momentum

WHAT DRIVES MARKETS DOWN:
  - Recession fears
  - Rising interest rates (discounts future earnings at higher rates, reducing valuations)
  - Geopolitical crises
  - Credit market stress (banks failing, bond defaults)
  - Corporate earnings disappointments
  - Inflation eroding consumer spending power

KEY CORRELATION — STOCKS VS BONDS (THE TRADITIONAL RELATIONSHIP):
For decades, stocks and bonds had a negative correlation — when stocks fell (in 
recessions), bonds rose as central banks cut rates. This made a portfolio of both 
stocks and bonds "balanced." However, in 2022, both stocks AND bonds fell simultaneously 
because the threat was high inflation, which hurts both (stocks via rising rates, bonds 
directly because their fixed coupons lose value). This was an unusual and painful period 
for traditional "balanced" investors.

SECTOR 5: BOND MARKETS (GOVERNMENT AND CORPORATE BONDS)
================================================================
Bonds have an inverse relationship with interest rates. When rates rise, existing 
bond prices fall (because new bonds offer higher yields, making old bonds less 
attractive). When rates fall, existing bond prices rise.

The US 10-year Treasury yield is the most important interest rate benchmark in the 
world. It affects mortgage rates, corporate borrowing costs, and asset valuations 
globally. When the US 10-year yield rises, it:
  - Raises borrowing costs globally
  - Attracts capital from emerging markets into US bonds
  - Puts pressure on growth stocks and real estate values
  - Strengthens the USD

In 2022-2023, the US 10-year yield rose from near 0% to over 5% — the fastest rate 
rise in 40 years. This caused bond market losses, stock market corrections, real estate 
slowdowns, and emerging market currency pressures simultaneously.

SECTOR 6: AGRICULTURAL COMMODITIES (WHEAT, RICE, EDIBLE OILS)
================================================================

For India and most developing nations, agricultural commodity prices directly affect 
the cost of living for the largest number of people. The key drivers are:

  - Monsoon and rainfall patterns (for India, Southeast Asia)
  - El Niño / La Niña weather cycles (affect global agricultural output every few years)
  - Russia-Ukraine conflict: Ukraine produces 10% of global wheat and 50% of global 
    sunflower oil. Russia is the world's largest wheat exporter. Their war disrupted 
    both supply and created a global food price crisis in 2022.
  - Palm oil supply: Indonesia and Malaysia produce 85% of global palm oil. 
    Export restrictions or production disruptions there affect cooking oil prices worldwide.
  - Fertilizer prices: Fertilizers are made from natural gas and phosphate rock. 
    Russia and Belarus are major exporters of both. The war disrupted fertilizer supply, 
    raising crop production costs globally.

Agricultural commodities have a POSITIVE correlation with inflation, a WEAK correlation 
with gold (both are "real assets"), and a NEGATIVE correlation with drought-hit currencies.

SECTOR 7: REAL ESTATE
================================================================
Real estate prices are driven by:
  - Local demand-supply (most important factor — real estate is fundamentally local)
  - Interest rates (higher rates = higher EMIs = reduced affordability = less demand)
  - Economic growth and employment levels
  - Infrastructure development (metro, highways near a property)
  - Population growth and urbanisation

Correlation with other assets: Real estate has LOW correlation with equities in the 
short term, making it a useful diversifier. However, in severe economic downturns 
(2008, for example), real estate and equities can fall together as credit dries up.

In India (2023-2025), residential real estate prices have been rising in tier-1 and 
tier-2 cities despite high home loan rates, driven by pent-up demand post-COVID, 
rising incomes in the salaried class, and limited supply in premium segments.
